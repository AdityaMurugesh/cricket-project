## 7. New: why the tolerant delivery is flat
- Show: `results/kinematic/equifinal_mechanism.png`
- Left panel: the noise-trained landing curves sit inside the good-length band for tens of milliseconds.
  The noise-free one cuts straight through it.
- Right panel: releasing later does two things. The ball is going faster (lands further, green) and it has
  turned downward (lands shorter, blue).
  - Tolerant bowlers: the two nearly cancel: +1.4 to +2.1 m against −1.9 to −2.8 m, net 0.1–1.0 m per 10 ms.
  - Brittle bowlers: no cancellation: +0.4 to +1.2 m against −4.4 to −10.3 m, net 3.8–8.8 m per 10 ms.
- Say: this is Müller & Loosch's equifinal path, shown inside one delivery. The arm swings about half as fast
  (800–925 against 1400–1800 deg/s) and releases 4–14° upward (brittle: about −2 to +2°), so the speed gain
  can match the elevation loss.
- Don't quote the noise-free covariation ratios (up to 45): the length SD there is 5 mm, so the ratio is
  meaningless. Under torque noise it is 0.88–2.10, so mixed.

## 8. New: the real bowler's hand looks like the brittle bowlers
- Show: `results/kinematic/tolerance_vs_pace.png` (stars), and the orange curves in item 7
- **Say first, this is not a measured landing:** there is no ball in the capture. It's where a ball would land
  if it left with the hand or wrist marker's velocity.
- Say: at the moment that landing crosses good length, the real hand moves it 4.6–7.1 m per 10 ms at 55–72 km/h.
  It stays in the zone for 2.5–4.5 ms, and would be good only 8–15% of the time under 12 ms jitter. Almost no
  speed/elevation cancellation, released near flat (−2 to +6°). That's the same place as the noise-free policies.
- So the tolerant delivery is a real solution in the simulator, but this isn't how this bowler appears to do it.
  Either real release timing is much tighter than 12 ms, or fingers and wrist do something a hand marker can't
  see, or this bowler's length really does scatter. We can't tell which from this data.
- **Weak points:** one non-elite bowler, 4 deliveries, 100 Hz, 2 of the 4 are wrist markers. The marker's own
  speed peak would land at only 4–6 m, so the ball must leave earlier (12–26 ms by this estimate) or get
  extra from the fingers.

## 10. If he asks about the cricket papers
- **Lindsay et al. 2024** (J Sports Sci 42:708, swing bowling, elite Australian fast bowlers): the pelvis and
  trunk stay stable from delivery to delivery, and the bowling arm varies. That variation sets the seam for swing.
  - Our bowler has this structure by construction: the body is the fixed capture, only the arm varies. But the
    simulation has no seam, swing, wrist or fingers, which is what Lindsay's arm variation is for.
- **Andrews et al. 2024** (Eur J Sport Sci, 92 fast bowlers): delivery-stride timing varies more than speed.
  - Same pattern in the noise-free policies under torque noise: the arm's timing varies 1.6–3.4× more than speed.
    In the new runs it's not consistent: 4 of 6 still show it (1.1–3.2×), and a250 s1 and a290 s1 reverse (0.6×).
  - A CV comparison shows the pattern, not that timing variation *causes* the steady speed. Andrews infers it
    the same way.
  - Our captured bowler doesn't show it (run-up timing CV 0.03–0.05, speed CV 0.04–0.07). One non-elite bowler,
    so a limitation, not a finding.
