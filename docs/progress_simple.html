The cricket project, explained simply

What we did since the 6 Nov meeting. Read it top to bottom once, and you can explain it. The green boxes are lines you can say as they are. The full version, with every number, is docs/progress_since_6nov.pdf.

The whole story in one paragraph

Our computer bowler could be fast or accurate, never both. We found out why. To land a fast ball on a good length, you have to let go of it at almost exactly the right moment, within about a thousandth of a second. A slow ball is much more forgiving. So pace depends on timing skill: a bowler with perfect timing can bowl fast and accurately, but a bowler with ordinary timing can't. We then got the RL bowler to reach that limit. Finally, we made the simulation more realistic and checked that the answer stays the same.

1Where we were on 6 Nov

Our bowlers were either fast but unreliable, or reliable but slow.

  • The bowler's body is a real person's motion capture, played back exactly. Only the bowling arm is controlled by the learning program (RL, specifically PPO).
  • A good ball means three things: a legal arm (elbow straightens 15° or less), it pitches on a good length (10.9–12.9 m), and it stays on line.
  • Real people can't let go of the ball at exactly the same moment every time. We add that wobble on purpose: the ball leaves a few milliseconds early or late at random.
  • With that wobble, our bowlers were accurate at only 45–55 km/h. The faster ones missed most of the time.

The open question: is that the arm's real limit, or is the learning program just not good enough?

2We stopped tuning and asked the physics directly

We used a second method to work out the best ball the arm can possibly bowl.

Think of two people. A coach (RL) teaches a bowler through thousands of practice balls. An engineer (an optimiser called CMA-ES) takes one ball and tweaks every muscle command until it is as fast as possible. The engineer's answer is the ceiling: the best the coach could ever hope for.

We asked the engineer: "What is the fastest ball that is still good 25% of the time? 50%? 90%?"

Answer: with perfect timing the arm can reach about 113–130 km/h. As soon as you ask for reliability, the speed falls fast. Our reliable RL bowlers were already sitting on that ceiling.

Say: "Our slow, reliable bowlers weren't a training failure. An independent optimiser shows that this is the physical limit for that level of reliability."

3Why: the "launch window"

The faster the ball, the less time you have to let go of it.

The hand moves in a circle. If you let go a tiny bit late, the ball points a little more downwards. If you let go early, it points more upwards. For a slow ball that barely matters. For a fast ball, a tiny change in angle moves the landing spot a long way. On top of that, a faster arm turns further in the same instant.

BallHow precisely you must let go
about 100 km/hwithin about 1 ms
about 55 km/hanywhere in a 20–60 ms window

The research literature on throwing says the same: accurate fast throws need about 1 ms precision (Calvin 1983; Chowdhary & Challis 1999).

Say: "Fast bowling is a timing problem. A 100 km/h ball must leave the hand within about one millisecond to pitch on a length. That's physics, and it matches the throwing research."

4The main result: pace is a timing skill

The same arm bowls fast and accurately only if its timing is very good.

We asked: "If a bowler's release timing wobbles by X milliseconds, what is the fastest ball that still pitches on a good length 90% of the time?"

5Getting the RL bowler up to the limit

Three tricks took the RL bowler from 77 km/h to 110 km/h, and onto the limit at every timing level.

  1. Start from the engineer's answer. RL learning from nothing got stuck at 77 km/h. So we first copied the optimiser's fast ball into the RL network (this is called behaviour cloning), then let RL improve it. To make one plan work every time, the arm follows the real person's motion until 0.5 s before release, then the network takes over (the arm handover).
  2. Make RL experiment less. RL learns by trying slightly random moves. For a ball that must be timed to 1 ms, those random moves make it miss, so RL "plays safe" and bowls slower (the copied ball dropped from 106 to 85 km/h). Turning the randomness down kept the pace. Result: 104 km/h, and 110 km/h in the newer, more realistic model.
  3. Tell RL how reliable to be. Our reward quietly preferred very safe, slow balls. We added a setting that says, for example, "aim for 75% good". The bowlers then got 15–23 km/h faster at the reliability we asked for, matching or slightly beating the optimiser.

We also made the training signal less random. Instead of "hit or miss" for each ball, the simulator now works out the exact chance of a good ball and pays that.

Say: "RL from scratch stalls at 77 km/h. It's a starting-point problem, not a training-time problem. We start RL from the optimiser's ball, lower its exploration noise, and tell it how reliable to be. Then it reaches the physical limit."

For the air and elbow tests we ran a fair comparison: old model and new model with identical settings. On average they differ by only 0.2 km/h.

We also found and fixed a small measuring bug. The simulator works in 2 ms steps, which made very good timers look a bit better than they are. All the numbers here use the fixed version.

Say: "We made the model more realistic and re-ran everything with a fair control. The answer didn't move, so it isn't an artefact of our simplifications."

7A surprise: a forgiving fast-ish ball

The optimiser found an 83 km/h ball that still lands on a good length when it's released anywhere in a 26 ms window.

That should be impossible by section 3. The trick: when this ball is released early, it comes out slower and at a different angle, and the two mistakes cancel out, so it still lands in the same place. Different releases, same result. This is exactly the equifinality idea your supervisor asked about.

The catch: the early releases are slower balls (down to 47 km/h). They're "good" because they land on a length, not because they're fast. It also only works about half the time. The RL bowler hasn't found this ball yet.

8Honest limits

  • One person. All the motion comes from one participant (four of their deliveries).
  • Only the arm learns. The body is played back. A real fast bowler gets a lot of pace from the trunk and front leg.
  • Simple ball flight. Air resistance only: no swing, seam or bounce.
  • The optimiser's answers are "at least this good". A better search can find faster balls, which is why RL sometimes beats it slightly.

9If they ask…

"Is 12 ms of timing wobble realistic?"

Yes for a normal bowler. Measured human release timing varies by 3–15 ms. Elite throwers are estimated at 1–2 ms. That's why we give results for every timing level.

"How do you know RL isn't just bad?"

A completely different method (the optimiser), using the same simulator and scoring, gives the same limit, and our RL bowlers reach it.

"What's new compared with other work?"

Earlier bowling simulations find one perfect, noise-free fastest ball. We find the fastest ball when timing is imperfect, under the legal elbow rule, and show RL can reach it.

"Does the 15° law cost pace?"

Not in our model. Taking the law away found nothing faster.

"Isn't this just one person's quirk?"

Four different recorded run-ups give the same pattern. It's still one person, so more people are the next step.

"What next?"

Motion capture from more bowlers (ideally elite ones), letting the trunk move too, putting a marker on the ball in the lab to measure real release timing, and a write-up.
