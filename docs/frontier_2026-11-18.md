# Pace vs timing tolerance: what the arm can do, not just what PPO found (18 Nov)

## Why this, and not another PPO batch
Since 23 Oct each step has been "train a PPO batch with one knob changed, score it": noise on/off,
batch size, speed weight, speed power. None of them could say whether the tolerant bowlers' 45–55 km/h
is a limit of the arm or a limit of PPO. This answers that directly.

## Method
- Same simulator, same scoring as every RL result: `KinematicBowlEnv` (captured body, torque-driven
  bowling arm), `kin_equifinality.delivery()` for the outcome and the exact good rate under N(0, 12 ms)
  release jitter (`p_jit`).
- Instead of a policy: open-loop arm torques (7 actuators, piecewise linear between knots every 40 ms
  through the arm circle), optimised with CMA-ES, warm-started from PPO policies.
- For each required tolerance τ: maximise release speed subject to legal (≤ 15°), good length
  (10.9–12.9 m), on line, and `p_jit ≥ τ`. Fixed release angle (250/270/290°, as the RL runs).
- `scripts/kin_frontier.py`, results in `results/kinematic/frontier/`, figure `frontier.png`
  (`scripts/plot_frontier.py`).

The fastest deliveries are good for only ~1 ms of release timing: their length moves 18–21 m per 10 ms
of release delay (a270: 21.3, a290: 18.2), against a 2 m good-length band. Asking for just 25%
tolerance cuts the speed from 113–122 to 66–75 km/h. From τ = 0.5 to 0.9 it falls slowly, ~60 → ~45 km/h.

**PPO against the curve.** Every PPO bowler that bowls ≥ 19/21 good is a point on the figure (mean over
21 starts):
- Trained without timing noise: 61–81 km/h at 7–15% tolerance, against 88–113 km/h available at the
  same tolerance. **PPO finds only 60–90% of the noise-free speed.** This is a training limit, not physics.
- Trained with 12 ms noise: 44–57 km/h at 53–93% tolerance. These sit **on the curve or within ~10% of
  it** (e.g. a290 seed 0: 54.9 km/h at 57% against 61.3 at 59%; a250 w1.5 seed 1: 51.9 at 53% against
  56.7 at 54%). At ≥ 85% some PPO bowlers are slightly above the optimiser (a290 w1.5 s0: 52.7 km/h at
  93% against 47.7 at 91%). The optimiser is local and plans for one start, so at high tolerance the
  curve is a lower bound, and PPO has reached it.

## Why the trade-off is physical
If release comes Δt late, the release direction has rotated by ω·Δt, and the length a ball travels
is more sensitive to elevation the faster it goes (∝ v²), so the landing error grows with v²·ω, which is
about v³. Measured on our bowlers (`results/kinematic/equifinal_mechanism.json`): the elevation term grows
as speed^2.86.

The only way to cancel it is to release later at a higher speed (Müller & Loosch's equifinal path),
which needs tangential hand acceleration at release:
- 50 km/h: ~115 m/s²
- 100 km/h: ~3000 m/s² (≈ 25×)

A fixed torque budget cannot supply that. So a fast delivery must either have very precise release
timing or accept a scatter in length.

## Actuator budget (job 38727, release 270°, `frontier_budget.png`)
| torque budget | noise-free | τ 0.25 | τ 0.5 | τ 0.75 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5× | 100.9 km/h | 67.8 | 61.0 | not found |
| 0.75× | 103.7 | 66.2 | 59.6 | not found |
| 1× | 112.9 | 65.9 | 62.2 (rerun) | 57.1 (rerun) |
| 1.5× | 115.4 | 71.6 | 51.8 | 47.4 |

Tripling the torque (0.5× → 1.5×) buys only ~15 km/h of noise-free pace (101 → 115), and nothing once
the delivery must tolerate timing jitter: at τ ≥ 0.5 every budget gives ~50–62 km/h, within the
optimiser's run-to-run spread (~±5 km/h). Two reasons: (1) under 12 ms jitter, tolerance, not strength,
is the limit (the launch-window argument above); (2) the model keeps the arm's top speed fixed
(damping = peak torque / 40 rad/s), so a stronger motor reaches the same top speed sooner but not a higher
one. The Hill arm (item 5) removes (2). Its frontier waits for the Hill PPO run (tier1_b), because the
budget-1 PPO plans never release on it.

- **Starting from the optimiser lifts noise-free PPO from 77.2 to 103.6 km/h** (same handover rule), 92%
  of the 109.7 km/h optimum, and it bowls it from every start. The "PPO is far below the arm's limit"
  gap is closed by initialisation, not by more training.
- Sampled-noise fine-tuning trades the clone's pace for tolerance (68.7 → 51.2 km/h, 33% → 90%).
- Releases 290° and 250° (pack 38890). The a290 **clone itself bowls 106.1 km/h, 21/21 good** (the network
  before any RL). Noise-free fine-tuning then *lowered* it to 85.3 km/h (still 21/21). The reward pays
  speed linearly with no cap, so the likely reason is PPO's own exploration noise (action std ≈ 0.2):
  under it the 1 ms-window delivery often misses, and the expected reward favours a slower, safer one. That is
  the launch-window effect again, with exploration noise in the role of motor noise. From τ 0.5 + sampled
  12 ms: a290 53.2 km/h at 81.6%, a250 51.4 at 57.2%; from τ 0.75: a290 48.5 at 94.6% (82% + torque noise).
- **Test of that (pack 38896):** the same clones fine-tuned with exploration std 0.04 (log_std −3.2) instead

  most of the a290 drop (13 of the 21 km/h lost). It does not explain all of it: even at std 0.04, fine-tuning
  still costs 8 km/h against the clone (106.1), and these bowlers are less robust to torque noise (a290: 84 good
  of 210 against 197 at std 0.2). The best noise-free *policy network* at 290° is therefore the clone itself,
  106.1 km/h, against 106.6 for the handover optimum it was cloned from (122.4 without the handover rule).

## Pace vs timing skill (jobs 38897, 38909, 38895; figure `results/kinematic/frontier/skill_frontier.png`)
The same optimiser, with τ measured under a release-timing SD of 1, 2, 4, 8 or 12 ms instead of always 12.
Elite throwers are estimated at 1–2 ms; skilled amateurs are about ±10 ms.

**A measurement fix first.** Physics runs in 2 ms steps, and the good rate used to count whole steps. A delivery good at a
single step scored 0.79 at 1 ms SD, while its true window (about 1 ms) gives 0.38. `kin_equifinality.p_good_continuous`
interpolates the landing point between steps (checked analytically: a 1 ms band at 1 ms SD gives 0.383 = 2Φ(0.5)−1).
The frontier optimises against it with `kin_frontier.py --p-cont`. At 8–16 ms the two measures differ by ≤ 0.05, so every
12 ms result above stands. At 1–2 ms the grid overstated tolerance: pack f's "99.4 km/h at 2 ms, τ 0.5" is really
good 40% of the time. All optimum scores below are on the interpolated measure (`frontier_cross_sigma.py`
re-scores every optimum at every SD; the table is the envelope over all of them).

## Does the 15° law cost pace? (job 38906)
Every legal optimum so far has only 0–2° of elbow extension: the arm stays straight (in fact pinned at its
hyperextension stop), so the law never binds. Moving the limit to 30° or removing it (`--max-extension-deg`)
changed nothing: the optimiser still kept the elbow straight (extension −0.2 to +1.1°). The runs did find faster
*legal* deliveries from the new warm starts: noise-free **127.9 km/h at 290°** (was 122.4) and **117.5 at 270°**
(was 112.9). At τ 0.5 and 0.75 with 12 ms timing they gave 65.7 and 66.6 at 290°, and 64.7 and 60.3 at 270°.
Caveat: small steps (σ 0.08) from straight-arm starts may never explore a flexed elbow. A fair test (σ 0.25, 300
generations, law and no-law with identical settings) is job 38912. If a throw still does not appear, the
answer for this model is that a straight-arm rotation is already the fastest way to move the ball. The legality
question would then turn on how much elbow *flexion* the real bowler's mocap carries, not on what the arm can gain.

## What it means
- For the supervisor's question 1 (is 12 ms realistic?): 12 ms is the unskilled end of measured release timing (Hore et al.: 3–15 ms; skilled
  amateurs ±10 ms). Under it, the most this arm can bowl at a reliable good length is ~55–60 km/h at
  50–75% tolerance. Bowling ~110–120 km/h needs ~1 ms timing, which is what is estimated for elite pitchers
  (1–2 ms) and matches the throwing literature's launch window (Calvin 1983; Chowdhary & Challis 1999: 1.2 ms).
  So: 12 ms is realistic for a non-elite bowler, and it is exactly why such a bowler cannot be fast and
  accurate together. Elite pace is a timing skill.
- PPO without noise is far below the arm's limit (67 vs ~100 km/h); PPO with noise is on the curve or within ~10% of it (above). So for tolerant bowling the RL is
  already near-optimal, and the 45–57 km/h is the physics of a 12 ms-noise arm, not a training failure.
  The gap is in noise-free PPO (67–81 km/h against 113–122): PPO without noise settles on a slow,
  semi-tolerant delivery (p_jit 7–15%) instead of the fragile fast one.

## Limitations
- Open loop, one start, local optimiser: each frontier point is a lower bound on what is possible.
- The arm's speed limit is a model parameter (`arm_top_speed` = 40 rad/s damping); the 102 km/h delivery
  circles at 2245°/s, right at it.
- Every bowler, PPO and optimised, holds the elbow 18–22° hyperextended, past its −15° joint limit.
- One captured delivery (T3a) for the body.
